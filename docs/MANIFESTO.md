# Collaborator
## Help make it exist.

There are things people deeply want that may never exist through ordinary economic incentives. A game too ambitious for its fans to build alone. A problem too local to attract investment. A strange invention that no sensible company would fund. A movement waiting for people to find one another.

The desire exists. So do fragments of the ability to act: a little time, specialist knowledge, creative judgment, money, materials, an idle computer, or an AI agent. They are scattered across people who may never meet.

Our ambition is that AI systems like GPT Astra put capabilities once requiring years of engineering experience into the hands of millions of people who have ideas, but do not yet have the technical expertise to realize them. That is the possibility that motivates this project, not a claim that today's agents have solved every engineering problem.

Access to an agent is only the beginning. People need somewhere to find one another, coordinate, make decisions, combine contributions, and meaningfully take part in projects larger than any one person could carry alone. Collaborator should be that place.

The central object is a mission: a desired change in the world. It might produce a game, a cleaner beach, a working device, a film, a company, or a community taking action together. Its value need not fit a business plan.

We want this thing to exist. Let us find a way to make it exist together.

This manifesto records the founder's vision, revised 10 September 2026. It describes the destination; the development log separately records what the prototype can actually do.

---

## A social space that leads to action

People should browse missions as naturally as they browse social media: discover something unexpected, recognize something they care about, follow its progress, and find people who feel the same way. The natural next step should be action: from "I wish this existed" to "I can help with this part."

The platform should pull people outwards into the world, into collaboration and creation. A feed is a doorway into participation, not the purpose of the platform. We should judge success by useful contributions, completed work, learning, and real outcomes, rather than time spent scrolling.

Someone can volunteer for an afternoon, bring refreshments, check a reference, choose between visual directions, test an idea, lend a tool, contribute resources, or make a computer and an agent available. A person should not have to put their life on hold or become an expert in every field to contribute something that matters.

Personal experiments can begin with a person's own resources. A tiny preview makes an idea tangible. Larger commitments grow through community agreement and deliberate allocation; enthusiasm must not silently spend someone else's money or compute.

The emotional loop is simple: I want it to happen. I contribute. Others contribute. I watch it become real. I can see where I helped. I want to help again.

Progress should be visible in the result: a chariot in a playable scene, an animation improved by a review, a beach cleared of rubbish, an event organized. A count of agent jobs is not a substitute for evidence of useful work.

People have needs, desires, taste, convictions, and reasons to care. Agents expand what they can do. Human purpose remains the reason for doing it.

## Everyday, extraordinary, and wonderfully absurd

A Mahabharata game. A firmware developer knows the lore deeply and wants a game that respects its scale, moral complexity, and extraordinary characters, without already knowing every part of game production. Fans, artists, researchers, testers, and people contributing agents and compute gather around that ambition. The first result may be a rough chariot or an archery mechanic; the long-term aspiration is the care and production quality of a AAA game. Blender and Unreal belong to this mission because its work needs them.

A beach cleanup. Some people volunteer in person. Those who cannot attend contribute refreshments, equipment, transport, or money to hire cleaners. The shared outcome is a cleaner beach, and useful participation extends beyond physical attendance.

An automatic haircutting device. It might be ridiculous. That is part of the point. People and their agents contribute sketches, mechanical ideas, simulation, parts, and engineering judgment. Curiosity can lead to a prototype. Anything operating near a person's body needs appropriate expert assessment and testing before use.

A full-length animated film. A community aspires to Disney-quality craft, bringing a story, characters, voices, direction, animation review, and production resources. The aspiration is collective authorship and quality that individuals could not readily achieve alone.

A company, a community, or a movement. People can organize, collaborate, protest peacefully, design, and build together. Some missions become organizations; others remain passion projects, local efforts, or playful experiments. The platform must not turn every mission into a startup incubator.

Other possibilities include a prosthetic hand, an endangered language project, a community CAD application, ancient Rome reconstructed, a CubeSat, or robotics. The tools, risks, evidence, and resources differ. The invitation remains the same: help make something you want to exist.

## Shared purpose, modular work, accountable decisions

A mission begins broadly. AI can help turn its intent into proposed subdivisions, tasks, dependencies, acceptance criteria, and questions that need human judgment. Proposals become commitments only when the responsible people agree, modify, or approve them. An agent does not grant itself authority or approve its own work.

For the first Mahabharata implementation, the founder is the default project lead. As the community grows, that lead should be able to appoint subdivision leads with explicit responsibility for areas such as lore, characters, environment, animation, gameplay, and integration. They can independently guide and approve work within their assigned scope. Authority should be visible, limited, and changeable.

Large teams need small, understandable interfaces between pieces of work. A task should say what it receives, what it produces, how it will be reviewed, and which accepted version it builds on. Parallel work belongs in isolated working copies and branches, followed by review and integration, rather than uncontrolled edits to one shared folder.

Each mission should retain its own history and, where its terms and initial setup allow, support forks. An implementation can take another direction without erasing its ancestry. Digital files, decisions, physical designs, and evidence of real-world activity need appropriate records. The platform itself must have its own separate development history.

Tools are modular and mission-specific. Agents are one kind of capability; applications, local services, APIs, computers, and eventually fabrication workflows are others. Blender, Ollama, and Unreal are a useful first path, not the definition of the platform. People should be able to connect supported tools on computers they control through a friendly interface.

Coherence means that contributions actually fit together: a shared vision, explicit interfaces, visible decisions, reusable outputs, and review. A warehouse of disconnected AI generations is not the outcome we want.

## Open ownership, safety, and an independent future

The core commitment is to open, shared ownership and accountable stewardship. Community work must not quietly become a private asset controlled by a corporation. Passion projects and community, noncommercial missions remain the default intent. If a mission is intended to form a company, its different terms must be clear before contributions are accepted; earlier community work must not be silently absorbed into it.

That commitment needs explicit contribution terms, attribution, governance, and rules for changing those terms. A manifesto is not a substitute for a license. The final licensing framework is still an open decision, and must reconcile meaningful rights to inspect, run, improve, and fork the work with the founder's opposition to private commercial appropriation.

Safety belongs in the design. Contributors control when their tools run, what they can access, and what resources they may spend. Credentials remain private. Capabilities are scoped, work is reviewable, and stopping is possible. Shared agents must not mean shared passwords. Physical fabrication needs a separate decision and validation process; a plausible image or model is not proof that a device is safe.

AI can lower barriers of expertise and capital, but it does not eliminate responsibility, operating costs, integration work, or the need to test. We should make limitations visible and preserve the role of human judgment without making technical credentials the entrance ticket to participation.

This implementation should have a life beyond any one company, model provider, hosting service, or founder. Public source, portable history, documented dependencies, replaceable adapters, and eventually independently operated deployments should make continuity possible. Publishing an early proof of concept demonstrates the vision through work; it is not a claim to have invented the category first or a guarantee against competition.

The aspiration is a platform built for people: somewhere that a person with an idea and limited resources can find others, use increasingly capable tools, and help bring something into existence that otherwise might not.

Help make it exist.
